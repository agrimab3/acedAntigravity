# ACED English — 5 ACT-style passages (final draft for import)

Written to real ACT English rules. 50 questions, 10 per passage.

## Format key (for importing)

- `[n]<u>text</u>` = underlined portion for question n. The text shown is exactly what appears in the passage (it may contain an error).
- `[n]` alone (no underline) = a point in the passage that question n refers to (add a sentence here).
- `(1)`, `(2)` … before sentences = sentence numbers, used only in paragraphs that have a placement or add/delete question. Show them in the passage.
- `[Paragraph 1]` labels = show as paragraph numbers.
- **Editing questions:** choice A is always NO CHANGE.
- **Add / delete / writer's goal / placement questions:** no NO CHANGE. Choices are Yes/No with reasons, or placement options.
- Every question has exactly one defensible answer.

# PASSAGE 1 — The Night Garden

[Paragraph 1]

On the edge of a busy neighborhood, a narrow strip of land lay between a parking lot and a brick wall. During the day, people hurried past it without looking. At night, however, the space became the setting for an unusual experiment. A group of volunteers wanted to discover whether a small garden could support moths and other insects that visit flowers after sunset.

[Paragraph 2]

The volunteers [1]<u>was</u> careful not to begin with a shopping trip. First, they spent two evenings observing the site. They recorded which lights stayed on, where rainwater collected, and how the wind moved between the buildings. [2]<u>Afterward; they</u> drew a plan that placed taller plants near the wall and shorter flowers beside the sidewalk. The plan left out a bright decorative lamp, [3]<u>since</u> the volunteers knew that artificial light could disturb the insects they hoped to observe.

[Paragraph 3]

Choosing flowers required more research than anyone expected. Some plants opened their blossoms in the morning; others released their strongest scent at dusk. The group selected several native species [4]<u>which blooms</u> at different times so that the garden would offer food throughout the season. Instead of replacing all the soil, the volunteers tested it and added compost only where it was needed. This slower approach [5]<u>was something that resulted in the saving of</u> money and materials.

[Paragraph 4]

The first week brought little excitement. A few flowers drooped in the heat, and one volunteer wondered whether the experiment had failed. [6]<u>Similarly,</u> the group kept taking notes rather than making sudden changes. By the third week, observers had counted several kinds of moths and noticed tiny beetles moving among the leaves. [7]<u>Walking quietly between the beds, several insects were photographed by the volunteers.</u> Each visitor was recorded without being touched and then compared with pictures in a local field guide.

[Paragraph 5]

(1) The project did not prove that every vacant strip should become a garden. (2) Its results came from one small site observed during a single summer. [9] (3) Still, the volunteers had learned something useful: careful observation could turn an overlooked place into a source of questions. (4) They also invited neighbors to share what they noticed. (5) For their next step, they planned to repeat the count in autumn. (6) By opening their notebooks to the neighborhood, the volunteers made the project a shared investigation rather than a private experiment.

### Questions

**1.** (easy · subject-verb agreement)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. were
- C. has been
- D. being

**Answer: B**
- A: "Was" is singular, but the subject "volunteers" is plural.
- B: Correct. "Were" agrees with the plural subject and keeps the past tense.
- C: "Has been" is singular and shifts out of the past tense.
- D: "Being" cannot serve as the main verb of a sentence.

**2.** (easy · punctuation)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. Afterward, they
- C. Afterward: they
- D. Afterward, and they

**Answer: B**
- A: A semicolon must join two complete sentences; "Afterward" alone is not one.
- B: Correct. A comma sets off the introductory word.
- C: A colon must follow a complete sentence; "Afterward" is not one.
- D: "And" has nothing to join; "Afterward" is not a clause.

**3.** (easy · logical connector)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. although
- C. unless
- D. so

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. "Since" shows the reason the lamp was left out: light disturbs insects.
- B: "Although" signals contrast, but the second idea explains the first.
- C: "Unless" creates a condition that makes no sense here.
- D: "So" reverses cause and effect (the lamp being left out did not cause the volunteers' knowledge).

**4.** (medium · agreement in a clause)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. that blooms
- C. that bloom
- D. they bloom

**Answer: C**
- A: "Blooms" is singular, but it refers to "species," which is plural here ("several species").
- B: Still uses the singular "blooms."
- C: Correct. "That bloom" agrees with the plural "species" and correctly limits which species were chosen.
- D: Creates a run-on: "several native species they bloom" joins two clauses with no connector.

**5.** (medium · concision)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. ended up being a savings of
- C. was a saving for
- D. saved

**Answer: D**
- A: Wordy; "was something that resulted in the saving of" buries the action.
- B: Still wordy and awkward ("being a savings of money").
- C: Changes the meaning; the approach didn't save "for" money.
- D: Correct. "Saved money and materials" is direct and clear.

**6.** (medium · transitions)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. Consequently,
- C. Nevertheless,
- D. For instance,

**Answer: C**
- A: "Similarly" signals likeness, but continuing despite doubt is a contrast.
- B: "Consequently" suggests the doubt caused the group to keep going, which is illogical.
- C: Correct. "Nevertheless" shows the group kept working despite the discouraging first week.
- D: "For instance" introduces an example, but this sentence is not an example of the doubt.

**7.** (medium · modifier placement)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. Several insects were photographed walking quietly between the beds by the volunteers.
- C. The volunteers, several insects photographed, walking quietly between the beds.
- D. Walking quietly between the beds, the volunteers photographed several insects.

**Answer: D**
- A: The opening phrase "Walking quietly between the beds" seems to describe the insects, which is illogical.
- B: Still suggests the insects were walking, and the passive wording is awkward.
- C: Not a complete sentence; it has no main verb.
- D: Correct. "The volunteers" comes right after the phrase, so it's clear who was walking.

**8.** (hard · sentence placement)
For the sake of logic and cohesion, Sentence 4 in Paragraph 5 should be placed:
- A. where it is now.
- B. before Sentence 1.
- C. after Sentence 5.
- D. after Sentence 6.

**Answer: C**
- A: "Also" needs an earlier action to add to; Sentence 3 is about what they learned, not an action.
- B: Inviting neighbors makes no sense before the paragraph explains the project's limits.
- C: Correct. "Also" adds to the plan in Sentence 5, and Sentence 6 ("By opening their notebooks to the neighborhood") directly builds on the invitation.
- D: Sentence 6 already refers to involving neighbors, so the invitation must come before it.

**9.** (hard · add a sentence)
At point 9, the writer is considering adding the following sentence:
*"Moths are most active on warm, humid nights."*
Should the writer make this addition here?
- A. Yes, because it explains why the volunteers chose to observe in summer.
- B. Yes, because it supports the claim that the results came from one small site.
- C. No, because it contradicts the earlier statement that moths visited the garden.
- D. No, because it interrupts the discussion of what the project could and could not show with a general fact about moths.

**Answer: D**
- A: The paragraph is about the project's limits, not about why summer was chosen.
- B: The sentence says nothing about how many sites were studied.
- C: It doesn't contradict anything; moths did visit the garden.
- D: Correct. The paragraph weighs the project's results, and this general fact breaks that line of thought.

**10.** (medium · writer's goal)
Suppose the writer's goal had been to show how a small community project can produce useful knowledge even when its conclusions are limited. Would this essay accomplish that goal?
- A. Yes, because it shows volunteers carefully observing a small site and then admitting what their results could not prove.
- B. Yes, because it proves that every vacant strip of land should become a garden.
- C. No, because it focuses only on how the volunteers chose their plants.
- D. No, because the volunteers never record any observations.

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. The essay covers careful observation, real findings, and clearly stated limits.
- B: Paragraph 5 says the project did *not* prove this.
- C: Plant choice is only one paragraph; the essay also covers observation and results.
- D: The volunteers take notes, count moths, and photograph insects.

# PASSAGE 2 — The Last Mapmaker

[Paragraph 1]

When Mara joined the coastal survey team, she expected to spend most of her time drawing lines on maps. Instead, her first assignment involved standing in ankle-deep water and holding a measuring pole while a colleague recorded the position of the reeds. The marsh they were studying changed [1]<u>it's</u> shape after nearly every storm. A channel that appeared wide in spring might be almost invisible by late summer.

[Paragraph 2]

(1) Older maps showed the marsh as a fixed border between land and sea. (2) Mara soon realized that this border was anything but fixed. (3) Sediment moved with the tides, and plants slowed the water enough for new patches of ground to form. (4) At the same time, stronger waves could wash away a bank overnight. (5) [2]<u>For instance,</u> the team needed repeated measurements rather than a single perfect drawing.

[Paragraph 3]

Each morning, the researchers checked their instruments before setting out. They used satellite coordinates to locate their stations, [3]<u>but they also kept</u> handwritten notebooks. A battery could [4]<u>fail, however a</u> pencil could still record a measurement. Mara labeled each sketch with the date, the weather, and the tide level. Those details mattered because a line on a map meant little without the conditions [5]<u>under which it was drawn, recorded at the time it was drawn</u>.

[Paragraph 4]

(1) One afternoon, Mara found that a narrow island shown on last year's map had split into two pieces. (2) She was tempted to erase the old shape and replace it. (3) Her supervisor suggested another approach: preserve both outlines and note when each had been observed. (4) That way, future readers could see the marsh changing rather than mistake the difference for an error. (5) Mara began to think of maps not as finished portraits but as records of ongoing change.

[Paragraph 5]

At the end of the season, the team published a digital map with a timeline. Users could move a slider to compare different surveys, [7]<u>revealed</u> where the shoreline had shifted. Mara wrote a short note explaining the limits of the data: the team had measured selected locations, not every inch of the coast. [8] She hoped the map would help planners ask better questions [9]<u>rather then pretend</u> that the coastline would remain still.

### Questions

**1.** (easy · its / it's)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. its
- C. its'
- D. their

**Answer: B**
- A: "It's" means "it is," which doesn't fit ("changed it is shape").
- B: Correct. "Its" is the possessive form for the marsh.
- C: "Its'" is not a word.
- D: "Their" is plural, but "marsh" is singular.

**2.** (easy · transitions)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. Nevertheless,
- C. Similarly,
- D. Consequently,

**Answer: D**
- A: Repeated measurements aren't an example of erosion; they're a result of the constant change.
- B: "Nevertheless" signals contrast, but this sentence follows logically from the changes described.
- C: "Similarly" signals likeness, which doesn't fit.
- D: Correct. Because the marsh kept changing, the team needed repeated measurements.

**3.** (medium · sentence structure)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. but they also keeping
- C. they also kept
- D. but also keeping

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. "But" properly joins two complete clauses after the comma.
- B: "Keeping" can't be the main verb of the second clause.
- C: Creates a comma splice: two complete sentences joined only by a comma.
- D: "Keeping" leaves the second part without a proper verb and has no subject.

**4.** (medium · comma splice with "however")
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. fail however a
- C. fail; however, a
- D. fail, however, a

**Answer: C**
- A: Comma splice: "however" isn't a joining word, so a comma alone can't connect two sentences.
- B: Run-on with no punctuation at all.
- C: Correct. A semicolon joins the two sentences, and a comma follows "however."
- D: Still a comma splice; commas on both sides of "however" don't fix it.

**5.** (medium · redundancy)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. that were present and existed when drawing happened
- C. of drawing it at the time of its drawing
- D. under which it was drawn

**Answer: D**
- A: Redundant: "recorded at the time it was drawn" repeats "under which it was drawn."
- B: Wordy and redundant ("present and existed").
- C: Awkward and repetitive ("drawing … drawing").
- D: Correct. Clear and concise.

**6.** (medium · delete a sentence)
The writer is considering deleting Sentence 4 from Paragraph 4. Should the writer make this deletion?
- A. Yes, because it repeats the information in Sentence 3.
- B. Yes, because it contradicts Mara's later view of maps.
- C. No, because it explains why the supervisor's approach was useful.
- D. No, because it describes how the island split into two pieces.

**Answer: C**
- A: Sentence 3 gives the approach; Sentence 4 gives a new idea, its benefit.
- B: It supports Mara's view of maps as records of change; it doesn't contradict it.
- C: Correct. Without it, the reader doesn't learn why keeping both outlines matters.
- D: Sentence 1 describes the split; Sentence 4 doesn't.

**7.** (medium · verb form)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. revealing
- C. and it revealing
- D. which revealed them

**Answer: B**
- A: "Revealed" creates a second main verb with no subject or joining word.
- B: Correct. "Revealing" describes what comparing the surveys does.
- C: "It revealing" is not a complete verb.
- D: "Them" has nothing clear to refer to and makes the sentence ungrammatical.

**8.** (hard · add a sentence)
At point 8, the writer is considering adding the following sentence:
*"Satellite images, which show large areas at once, are often used to track coastlines."*
Should the writer make this addition here?
- A. Yes, because it explains why the team did not need field measurements.
- B. Yes, because it supports the note's point about the limits of the data.
- C. No, because it shifts the focus from Mara's note and her hopes for the map to a general statement about satellite images.
- D. No, because it contradicts the earlier statement that the team used satellite coordinates.

**Answer: C**
- A: The team clearly did take field measurements, so this reasoning is false.
- B: The sentence is about other methods, not about the limits of this team's data.
- C: Correct. It's a general fact that breaks the flow between the note and Mara's hope.
- D: There's no contradiction; using coordinates and using images are different things.

**9.** (easy · than / then)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. rather then pretending
- C. rather than pretended
- D. rather than pretend

**Answer: D**
- A: "Then" refers to time; comparisons use "than."
- B: Still uses "then."
- C: "Pretended" doesn't match "ask" (both should follow "would help planners").
- D: Correct. "Rather than pretend" matches "ask" and uses "than."

**10.** (hard · add a sentence / placement)
The writer wants to add the following sentence to Paragraph 2:
*"In other words, the coastline was growing and shrinking at the same time."*
The sentence would most logically be placed after Sentence:
- A. 1.
- B. 2.
- C. 3.
- D. 4.

**Answer: D**
- A: Nothing about growing or shrinking has been described yet.
- B: Mara has only said the border wasn't fixed; the two processes come later.
- C: Only growth (new ground forming) has been described so far.
- D: Correct. After Sentence 4, both growth (Sentence 3) and loss (Sentence 4) have been described, so "In other words" sums them up before the result in Sentence 5.

# PASSAGE 3 — A Library of Sounds

[Paragraph 1]

At the city museum, visitors could examine old cameras, typewriters, and kitchen tools. Yet curator Imani Brooks noticed that the displays were strangely quiet. A visitor could see a mechanical cash register but could not hear its bell. To Imani, something important was [1]<u>missing; the</u> sounds that had once accompanied ordinary work.

[Paragraph 2]

She proposed creating a sound archive. Rather than collecting recordings from the internet, the museum would invite people to bring working objects to a temporary studio. Staff members [2]<u>would record</u> each object in a quiet room, noting its age and the story of its owner. They began with a sewing machine, a bicycle bell, and a hand-cranked pencil sharpener. [3]<u>However,</u> the sewing machine made a steady clicking rhythm that changed whenever its operator increased the speed.

[Paragraph 3]

Recording was not as simple as pressing a button. Microphones picked up the hum of ventilation and the scrape of shoes against the floor. [4]<u>To reduce this noise, thick curtains covered the hard surfaces</u> and sessions were scheduled before opening hours. They also recorded a few seconds of silence in each session, allowing editors to identify background sounds. The process required patience [5]<u>more so than it required expensive equipment that cost a lot</u>.

[Paragraph 4]

(1) The archive soon raised questions that no photograph could answer. (2) Why did two apparently identical machines sound different? (3) Had their parts worn unevenly, or had their owners cared for them in different ways? (4) Instead of guessing, Imani asked a repair specialist to examine the machines. (5) The specialist explained how small changes in tension could alter the pitch of a mechanism. [7] (6) These observations [6]<u>added context to the recordings, making</u> the archive useful to researchers as well as visitors.

[Paragraph 5]

For the public exhibition, the museum installed listening stations beside selected objects. Each station offered a short recording and a caption describing how the recording had been made. Visitors could listen without operating fragile equipment. The museum also provided transcripts for spoken explanations and adjustable volume [8]<u>controls, features, that made</u> the exhibition more accessible. Imani hoped future visitors would recognize that history was not only something to look at; [9]<u>something people once heard every day as well</u>.

### Questions

**1.** (easy · colon vs. semicolon)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. missing: the
- C. missing the
- D. missing, and the

**Answer: B**
- A: A semicolon must be followed by a complete sentence; "the sounds that had once accompanied ordinary work" isn't one.
- B: Correct. A colon introduces an explanation of what was missing.
- C: Without punctuation, "missing the sounds" changes the meaning (Imani missing something).
- D: "And" has no second clause to join; what follows is not a complete sentence.

**2.** (easy · verb form)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. would recorded
- C. will recorded
- D. recording

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. "Would record" describes the planned action, matching "the museum would invite."
- B: "Would" must be followed by the base form "record."
- C: "Will" must be followed by "record," and "will" shifts the tense.
- D: "Recording" leaves the sentence without a main verb.

**3.** (easy · transitions)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. In conclusion,
- C. Instead,
- D. For example,

**Answer: D**
- A: "However" signals contrast, but the sewing machine detail illustrates the recordings.
- B: This isn't a conclusion; the passage continues.
- C: "Instead" suggests replacing something, which doesn't fit.
- D: Correct. The sewing machine is an example of the sounds recorded.

**4.** (medium · dangling modifier)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. Thick curtains, to reduce this noise, covering the hard surfaces
- C. To reduce this noise, the team covered the hard surfaces with thick curtains
- D. To reduce this noise, the hard surfaces were covered by thick curtains by the team

**Answer: C**
- A: The phrase "To reduce this noise" seems to describe the curtains, as if curtains decided to reduce noise. "They" in the next sentence then has no clear person to refer to.
- B: "Covering" leaves the clause without a main verb.
- C: Correct. "The team" is who reduced the noise, and it gives "They" in the next sentence a clear meaning.
- D: Wordy, passive, and repeats "by."

**5.** (medium · redundancy)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. more than costly and expensive equipment
- C. instead of equipment that was expensive and cost much
- D. rather than expensive equipment

**Answer: D**
- A: "Expensive" and "cost a lot" say the same thing, and the phrasing is wordy.
- B: "Costly and expensive" is redundant.
- C: Redundant and wordy.
- D: Correct. Clear and concise.

**6.** (medium · sentence structure)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. added context to the recordings; making
- C. added context to the recordings, they made
- D. adding context to the recordings, making

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. The main verb "added" is followed by a phrase ("making…") describing the result.
- B: A semicolon must be followed by a complete sentence; "making the archive useful…" isn't one.
- C: Comma splice: two complete sentences joined by only a comma.
- D: "Adding" leaves the sentence with no main verb.

**7.** (medium · add a sentence)
At point 7, the writer is considering adding the following sentence:
*"Tension refers to how tightly a spring or belt inside a machine is stretched."*
Should the writer make this addition here?
- A. Yes, because it defines a term that readers may not know and that is key to the specialist's explanation.
- B. Yes, because it shows that Imani repaired the machines herself.
- C. No, because it repeats information from Sentence 3.
- D. No, because it introduces a topic unrelated to the archive.

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. It clarifies "tension," which is central to why the machines sounded different.
- B: Nothing in the sentence says Imani did repairs; the specialist examined the machines.
- C: Sentence 3 is about wear and care, not tension.
- D: Tension directly explains the archive's question about different sounds.

**8.** (hard · commas with a restrictive clause)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. controls; features that made
- C. controls, features which making
- D. controls, features that made

**Answer: D**
- A: The comma after "features" wrongly separates "features" from the clause that defines them ("that made…").
- B: A semicolon must be followed by a complete sentence; "features that made…" isn't one.
- C: "Making" can't serve as the verb of the clause.
- D: Correct. One comma introduces the summary phrase, and "that made…" directly describes "features."

**9.** (medium · semicolon needs a full sentence)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. and it being something people once heard every day
- C. it was also something people once heard every day
- D. heard every day too

**Answer: C**
- A: After a semicolon you need a complete sentence; this has no verb.
- B: "It being" is not a complete verb.
- C: Correct. A complete sentence that finishes the contrast ("not only … also").
- D: A fragment with no subject.

**10.** (hard · writer's goal)
Suppose the writer's goal had been to describe how a museum used technology to replace its physical objects. Would this essay accomplish that goal?
- A. Yes, because the listening stations allowed the museum to remove its fragile equipment.
- B. Yes, because the archive was built from recordings found on the internet.
- C. No, because the essay describes recordings that add to the museum's objects rather than replace them.
- D. No, because the museum decided not to record any objects.

**Answer: C**
- A: The stations sit *beside* the objects; nothing was removed.
- B: The museum specifically chose *not* to use internet recordings.
- C: Correct. The recordings are placed next to the objects and add context; they don't replace them.
- D: The museum recorded many objects.

# PASSAGE 4 — The Bridge of Paper

[Paragraph 1]

The engineering club had only one afternoon to build a bridge. Their materials were modest: recycled paper, glue, string, and two wooden blocks that represented opposite riverbanks. The bridge needed to support a stack of small weights without touching the table below. At first, the students assumed that using more paper would automatically make [1]<u>there</u> design stronger.

[Paragraph 2]

Their teacher challenged that assumption by placing a flat sheet between the blocks. It bent under a single weight. Then she folded another sheet into a series of ridges. [2]<u>Although it used the same amount of paper. The folded sheet</u> supported several weights. The students realized that shape could matter as much as quantity, and they began testing different folds before building a full bridge.

[Paragraph 3]

One group rolled paper into narrow tubes, while another folded strips into triangles. Each team recorded the length of its pieces, the amount of glue used, and the weight [3]<u>that their structure could hold</u>. These measurements allowed the students to compare their designs fairly. When a tube collapsed, the students did not simply throw it away. [4]<u>Instead, they</u> inspected the crease where it had failed and adjusted the next version to spread the weight more evenly.

[Paragraph 4]

(1) As the deadline approached, disagreements became harder to avoid. (2) Some students wanted to reinforce every joint; others worried that extra glue would add weight and take too long to dry. (3) The reinforced joint held slightly more weight, [5]<u>but it also required</u> a longer drying time. (4) To settle the question, the club tested two otherwise identical joints, one with extra glue and one without it. (5) The result gave the team a trade-off to consider, not a universal rule.

[Paragraph 5]

In the final trial, the bridge held the required weights for a full minute. The students [7]<u>cheered then they carefully dismantled</u> the structure to examine its weak points. [8] They wrote down which choices had helped and which would need more testing. The next meeting would begin with a new question: could a lighter bridge support the same load? By the end of the afternoon, the club understood that engineering [9]<u>involved testing ideas, and then revising them, and doing this</u> in response to evidence. [10]<u>It had been a long afternoon.</u>

### Questions

**1.** (easy · there / their / they're)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. their
- C. they're
- D. its

**Answer: B**
- A: "There" refers to a place, not ownership.
- B: Correct. "Their" shows the design belongs to the students.
- C: "They're" means "they are."
- D: "Its" is singular, but it refers to the plural "students."

**2.** (easy · sentence fragment)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. Although it used the same amount of paper; the folded sheet
- C. It used the same amount of paper, the folded sheet
- D. Although it used the same amount of paper, the folded sheet

**Answer: D**
- A: "Although it used the same amount of paper." is a fragment; it can't stand alone.
- B: A semicolon can't follow a dependent "although" clause.
- C: Comma splice: two complete sentences joined by only a comma.
- D: Correct. The "although" clause is joined to the main sentence with a comma.

**3.** (medium · pronoun agreement)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. that it's structure could hold
- C. that its structure could hold
- D. its structure could have held

**Answer: C**
- A: "Their" is plural, but it refers to "each team" (singular), which already uses "its pieces."
- B: "It's" means "it is."
- C: Correct. "Its" matches "each team" and the earlier "its pieces."
- D: "Could have held" changes the meaning to something that didn't happen.

**4.** (medium · transitions)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. However, they
- C. Similarly, they
- D. In addition, they

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. "Instead" contrasts throwing the tube away with what they actually did.
- B: "However" contrasts awkwardly; the sentence offers an alternative action, which "Instead" expresses.
- C: "Similarly" signals likeness, but these are opposite actions.
- D: "In addition" suggests they threw it away *and* inspected it.

**5.** (medium · sentence structure)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. but it also requiring
- C. it also required
- D. but also it required that

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. "But" joins the two contrasting clauses after the comma.
- B: "Requiring" can't be the main verb of the second clause.
- C: Comma splice.
- D: Awkward word order, and "that" makes no sense before "a longer drying time."

**6.** (hard · sentence placement)
For the sake of logic and cohesion, Sentence 4 in Paragraph 4 should be placed:

**7.** (medium · run-on)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. cheered, then they carefully dismantled
- C. cheered; then carefully dismantling
- D. cheered and then carefully dismantled

**Answer: D**
- A: Run-on: two complete sentences with no punctuation or joining word.
- B: Comma splice: "then" isn't a joining word, so the comma can't join two sentences.
- C: After the semicolon, "then carefully dismantling" isn't a complete sentence.
- D: Correct. One subject ("The students") with two verbs joined by "and."

# PASSAGE 5 — Seeds on the Roof

[Paragraph 1]

During an especially hot September, students at Westbrook High School noticed that the top-floor classrooms felt warmer than the rooms below. Their science teacher suggested investigating the roof. Most of it was covered with dark material that absorbed sunlight. A small section, however, held planter boxes filled with herbs. The students [1]<u>wondered weather</u> the plants affected the temperature nearby.

[Paragraph 2]

They designed a simple study with two thermometers. One measured the air above the planted section; [2]<u>the other measured the air</u> above an exposed part of the roof. The students placed both instruments at the same height and checked them at identical times. By keeping those conditions consistent, they hoped to make the comparison fair. They also recorded cloud cover and wind speed, since weather could influence both readings. [3]<u>For several days the two areas showed, only small differences.</u>

[Paragraph 3]

Then a stretch of clear afternoons arrived. The planted area was often cooler, especially when the soil was moist. [4]<u>Therefore,</u> the students resisted announcing a dramatic discovery. Their sample was small, and the roof's different surfaces might have influenced the readings. They discussed how water evaporating from leaves can remove heat, [5]<u>a process that is called and known as</u> transpiration. The explanation was reasonable, but their measurements alone could not prove that it was the only cause.

[Paragraph 4]

(1) The class decided to improve its method. (2) They added more sensors and rotated them between locations to check whether one device read consistently high. (3) They also measured the temperature of the roof surface itself. [6] (4) Students graphed the readings, looking for patterns across several weeks rather than focusing on the hottest afternoon. (5) A longer record made their conclusions more cautious [7]<u>and more useful</u>.

[Paragraph 5]

At the school assembly, the students presented their results without claiming that every rooftop garden would have the same effect. [8]<u>There evidence came</u> from one roof during one season. They recommended continued monitoring and suggested that the school explore other benefits, including habitat for insects. [9]<u>The project had begun with a question about warm classrooms, it ended</u> with a lesson about measuring carefully before drawing conclusions.

### Questions

**1.** (easy · weather / whether)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. wondered whether
- C. wondered if weather
- D. wonder whether

**Answer: B**
- A: "Weather" means rain, sun, etc.; "whether" introduces a possibility.
- B: Correct. "Wondered whether" is the right word in the past tense.
- C: Still uses "weather."
- D: "Wonder" shifts to the present tense.

**2.** (easy · semicolon)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. , the other measured the air
- C. the other measuring the air
- D. and the other measured the air

**Answer: A**
- A: Correct. A semicolon correctly joins two complete, closely related sentences.
- B: Replacing the semicolon with a comma creates a comma splice.
- C: "Measuring" can't be the main verb after a semicolon.
- D: A semicolon shouldn't be followed by "and" here; use either the semicolon or ", and."

**3.** (easy · comma placement)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. For several days the two areas, showed only small differences.
- C. For several days, the two areas showed only small differences.
- D. For several days; the two areas showed only small differences.

**Answer: C**
- A: The comma wrongly separates the verb "showed" from what was shown.
- B: The comma wrongly separates the subject from its verb.
- C: Correct. The comma sets off the introductory phrase.
- D: A semicolon can't follow an introductory phrase.

**4.** (medium · transitions)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. For example,
- C. Similarly,
- D. However,

**Answer: D**
- A: "Therefore" suggests cooler readings caused the students to hold back, but the result would normally encourage a big claim.
- B: Holding back isn't an example of the cooler readings.
- C: "Similarly" signals likeness, not contrast.
- D: Correct. Despite encouraging results, the students stayed cautious.

**5.** (medium · redundancy)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. a process, it is called
- C. a process known as
- D. which processes are known as

**Answer: C**
- A: "Called and known as" says the same thing twice.
- B: Creates a comma splice ("it is called" is a full clause).
- C: Correct. Clear and concise.
- D: "Which processes" is plural and awkward.

**6.** (medium · add a sentence)

**7.** (medium · parallel structure)
- A. NO CHANGE
- B. and, more useful
- C. and usefulness
- D. and more usefully

**Answer: A**

**8.** (medium · there / their)
